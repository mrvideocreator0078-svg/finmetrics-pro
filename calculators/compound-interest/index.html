The Mathematics of Compounding: How Long-Term Wealth Accumulates

Compound interest is often referred to as the eighth wonder of the world for a simple mathematical reason: unlike simple interest, which only yields returns on the initial principal sum, compound interest generates earnings on both the initial principal and the accumulated interest from prior periods.

The Fundamental Future Value Formula
A = P (1 + r/n)^(nt) + PMT × [ ((1 + r/n)^(nt) - 1) / (r/n) ]
Where A is the final portfolio value, P is initial principal, PMT is monthly contribution, r is nominal annual interest rate, n is compounding frequency per year, and t is time in years.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a realistic annual rate of return to assume?
Historically, the S&P 500 index has generated an average annualized nominal return of approximately 10% over the last 90 years (before inflation). When adjusted for inflation, real returns have averaged between 6.5% and 7.0%. For conservative financial planning, most advisors recommend modeling returns between 7% and 9%.
How does compounding frequency impact returns?
The more frequently interest is calculated and added to the principal balance (daily vs. monthly vs. annually), the faster the balance grows. However, the difference between monthly and daily compounding is relatively minor due to the mathematical limits of continuous compounding.
What is the "Rule of 72"?
The Rule of 72 is a quick mental math shortcut to estimate how many years it takes for an investment to double at a given annual interest rate. Simply divide 72 by your interest rate. For example, at an 8% annual return, your money doubles in approximately 9 years (72 ÷ 8 = 9).
